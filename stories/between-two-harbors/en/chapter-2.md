I expected homesickness to arrive like weather. It came instead like a phone call from my mother asking whether I had eaten.

# The Phone Call Home

There was nothing dramatic in the conversation. She asked about work. I answered carefully. She asked whether I was sleeping enough. I lied. Then she told me the roof had leaked during the last rain, the market price of fish had dropped again, and the neighbor's youngest son had gotten accepted into a technical school.

I was halfway through saying congratulations when I realized I had stopped listening for the facts and started listening for the sound of home.

## What Travel Changes

Travel teaches you that distance is physical, but belonging is not.

I had assumed that leaving would loosen every thread. Instead, the opposite happened. The farther I went, the more clearly I could see which parts of me had been tied to that harbor all along: the way I looked at water first, the way I trusted people slowly, the way I still checked the sky before deciding anything important.

### The return

When I finally came back, nothing had waited politely for me. The roads had been repaired and repaired again. A few storefronts had changed hands. The harbor lights were newer, brighter, less romantic than my memory had made them.

But my mother was still in the kitchen. The kettle was still on the stove. And the coast, as always, was still doing what it had always done: arriving and leaving at the same time.
