**I was born with dark skin.**

Not the sun-kissed tan of children who spent their days running under the hot sun. **I was dark from the moment I left the womb**—so dark that my mother and the nurses in the delivery room stared at me in disbelief, speechless. People said I was **as dark as a rose myrtle berry** (*trái sim*). I grew up hearing that phrase without ever knowing what a rose myrtle actually was, until I recently searched for it online—it turns out to be a small, deep purple-black fruit that grows on hillsides. Well, that's fine; at least the comparison has a poetic, romantic touch.

The most common explanation in our household was: while pregnant with me, my mother had a severe inflammation. The doctor advised against taking traditional Chinese medicine. But the pain was too much to bear, so she took it anyway. And the result—I was born with that skin tone. As I grew older and did my own research, I found no scientific evidence that Chinese medicine affects a baby's skin pigmentation. But adults need a reason, and I understand that.

As for me, I think of it differently—perhaps the heavens gave me this skin **as a unique identity, something unmistakably mine**. No one around me wanted it—you know what I mean, haha—but **I never felt insecure because of it**.

What I heard even more often was the nickname the neighborhood people gave me: **Dân tộc** (The Ethnic Kid).

A name used to refer to people from the highlands, who spent their days under the elements and became dark. It sounds a bit discriminatory, but back then, I didn't understand any of it—to me, it was just a name people called me, like a nickname. **As a child, whatever people called me, I just answered and ran off to play again.**

**And boy, did I play a lot.**

Every day was the same—**out of the house in the morning, back only after dark**. The entire small neighborhood was a playground for my peers and me. Hide-and-seek, tag, chase—we played everything. The screaming, the laughter, the sound of running bare feet on the dirt road—that was **the soundtrack of a time when I didn't yet know what worry was**.

My closest playmate back then wasn't any of the neighbors, but my third brother—**my brother Quan**. We were exactly one year apart, so we grew up more like friends than brothers. Wherever one went, the other followed; whatever one played, we paired up. It was the kind of relationship where *if you saw one of us, the other was surely somewhere close by*.

**My family was poor**—something I understood gradually over the years, though I didn't feel it as a child. To a kid, being poor or rich doesn't matter as much as **having someone to play with**, being allowed to go outside, and having your mother sitting beside you when you come home at night. And I had all of those things.

**My parents loved me**—I felt that in a very clear way, even though no one ever said it out loud. Perhaps because I was the youngest, perhaps because I was always clingy, or perhaps because I was the kind of child who just brought joy to the adults. Regardless—I grew up enveloped in that love, and perhaps that is why **when I look back, my childhood memories are filled with light**.

I don't remember many details. A pre-schooler's memory is fragile like that—only a few scattered fragments remain: the dirt yard in front of the house, the voices of kids calling each other's names, my brother Quan's silhouette running ahead, and my mother's warmth when night fell.

But those brief fragments are enough for me to know: **I once had a very beautiful childhood**.

And then—I began to grow up a little. Big enough for them to lead me to a place called **kindergarten**.
